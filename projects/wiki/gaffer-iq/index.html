What is it?

The official Fantasy Premier League app rates every fixture on a scale of one to five. That number is attached to a team and left there: it cannot express that a side is brittle away and stubborn at home, that its form has turned since August, or that this particular attack is the wrong shape to hurt this particular defence.

Gaffer IQ replaces it. Difficulty is treated as a property of the pairing rather than of either team, built from five weighted signals, recomputed as the season moves, and — crucially — scored relative to the opponent, so the two ratings for a single fixture always sum to exactly 100.

It is a personal tool, not a product. No accounts, no sign-up, no multi-tenancy. A static frontend and exactly one serverless function.

Design Philosophy

Three commitments, made early and kept: the data is honest about where it came from, the engine is checkable against reality, and the interface shows its working rather than handing down a verdict.

The data

The governing rule is the proxy is dumb, the client is smart. Every piece of analytical logic runs in the browser. The one serverless function on Vercel exists solely because the FPL API sends no CORS headers — it fetches from an allowlist and forwards, and does nothing else.

FPL supplies the spine: squads, prices, positions, fixtures, per-player histories, live gameweek scoring. What it conspicuously does not supply is most of what makes a matchup readable. There is no standings endpoint, so the league table is accumulated from the fixtures feed instead. There is no access to any season but the current one. There are no minute timings, no goal-to-assist pairing, and no teamsheet of any kind.

Understat fills those gaps: expected goals for the shot-quality FPL never exposes, six seasons of cross-season fixture lists so head-to-head records can reach past August, chronological match timelines with minutes, and rosters carrying position codes and substitution linkage so a real starting XI and formation can be derived.((This is the one dependency outside FPL, and it is a deliberate trade — better evidence in exchange for a second thing that can break. Where a feed is missing, the match view degrades to FPL's grouped totals rather than failing.))

The engine

All scoring lives in engine/ and nowhere else. Modules consume scores; they never compute them. If a view needs a number the engine does not expose, the number is added to the engine and its breakdown rather than inlined at the point of use. The dependency chain runs strictly one way — modules to engine to normalise to store to api to proxy to FPL — and nothing points back up it.

Every tunable constant lives in config.js. Tuning the model means editing that file; it never means editing a formula. That separation is what makes the weights arguable rather than buried, and it is why they can be quoted on this page at all.

A rating you cannot check against last Saturday is a horoscope with extra steps.

Every metric function has to restate its direction in its own docblock, because a single inverted sub-metric quietly poisons the composite and is the hardest class of bug to see. The engine carries unit tests, and weights are treated as informed priors — documented so they can be disagreed with — until a season of stored pre-deadline scores can calibrate them empirically.

The interface

Five views, one engine underneath. None of them recomputes anything, so a rating can never disagree with itself between two screens. No framework, no bundler, no transpilation — files are served exactly as authored.

The app owns its entire document — its own head, fonts, tokens and stylesheets — and inherits none of jayjaybee.com's design system, exactly like [[fc26-calculator|the FC26 Feel Calculator]]. The only connection in either direction is the launch link at the top of this entry.((Sub-projects under /projects/apps/ are excluded from the site layout in _config.yml so this stays true even if one of them later grows front matter.))

Matchups

The deep dive on a single fixture: both sides scored, every sub-metric shown, each counter-matchup pairing openable to name the players behind it. It is the "view source" for any score anywhere else in the app — every other view links into it to answer why is this number what it is?

Scoring philosophy

Three ideas do most of the work.

Keep the signals separate. Each sub-metric is computed independently and reported independently, and they only combine at the very end. When a rating looks wrong you can see which signal is responsible instead of arguing with one opaque figure.

Score relative, not absolute. Every sub-metric is naturally computed against a fixed internal scale, with no reference to what the opponent scores on the same measure. Left there, two weak teams both rate well for facing a soft opponent, and two strong teams both rate badly for facing a tough one — neither of which says anything about who has the edge. So the composite is computed twice, once from each side, and the final value is derived from the difference between them. The two ratings for a fixture therefore sum to exactly 100 by construction rather than by coincidence.((The zero-sum property follows from a clamp identity rather than a tuning constant, so it holds at any edge sensitivity. It was verified by exhaustive sampling across the full input space including the clamping extremes.)) This flattens nothing: a genuine gap still produces a lopsided split, because the gap flows straight through the difference.

Don't let an unreliable reading vote. Sub-metrics report whether they are estimated. Estimated ones are excluded from the weighted sum entirely and the remaining weights are scaled back up to cover the full range. An earlier design let them pass through at a neutral 50, which meant a genuinely unreliable reading diluted the score at full weight while pretending to be information. The score is still always produced — never hidden — but a low-confidence one is flagged as provisional.

How they're calculated

Five weighted sub-metrics, summing to one:

  • Base difficulty — 0.40. Opponent strength priors. The largest weight because it is the only sub-metric that is never estimated: available from day one of a season, and it never degrades when player summaries or external data are missing.
  • Counter-matchup — 0.25. The signature metric, and most of what makes this score differ from FPL's own. One side's attacking pairings blended with its defensive ones, by position, so a team's attack and defence both earn credit on its own composite.
  • Team form — 0.20. Recent trajectory, opponent-adjusted. It exists to correct base difficulty's preseason-seeded priors in-season — it captures precisely what the largest metric structurally cannot.
  • Head-to-head — 0.10. Real cross-season meetings from the Understat history window. Kept deliberately small: it is not opponent-quality-adjusted, so a lopsided record mostly restates who has been the better side, which two larger metrics already say from far bigger samples.
  • Home and away — 0.05. Venue performance this season, held separately for both sides. The smallest of the five: real, but a clearer read rather than a bigger driver.

A stacking penalty then applies across the sub-metrics. It and the base-difficulty weight are a matched pair — a weight that large would otherwise make a strong favourite's score nearly immovable, and the penalty is what lets several poor secondary signals tip a fixture without letting any single one do it alone. Neither should be tuned without re-checking the other.

The result maps to five bands, symmetric around a neutral midpoint, which drive colour everywhere in the app: great 75–100, good 60–74, neutral 41–59, tough 26–40, brutal 0–25. Thresholds are config rather than literals, so the palette can be recalibrated after a season's distribution has actually been observed.

There used to be a sixth signal, style clash, and removing it is the most instructive decision in the model. It substantially restated the counter-matchup — two of its three rules described the same interaction, read from the same source data, at half the weight — and two correlated weak predictors are worse than one, because the shared noise gets counted twice while the dependable metrics are diluted. It also assumed season-average pressing is a stable team property, which it is not: a side presses a promoted team and sits off against a title contender. And its observed spread was narrow enough that it could move the composite by roughly half a point — too small to change a decision, large enough to add noise whenever it read a fixture wrong.((The style profiles themselves are still computed and still used elsewhere. It is the composite weight that was removed, not the code.))

Fixtures

The schedule-level companion to Matchups, and the one view that carries no scoring of its own. It answers what and when; the Matchup Analyser then explains it.

Four sub-views, all on live data. The gameweek grid gives kickoff times, results, per-fixture events and lineups. The league table is the one accumulated from the fixtures feed, with the European qualification and relegation zones each coloured separately. "By team" splits one club's season into results and upcoming. "Head-to-head" carries the full record for a pairing.

They cross-link throughout: a club name in the table or in a fixture row opens By team on that club, and an opponent in By team — or the head-to-head block inside any fixture, played or upcoming — opens Head-to-head on that pairing.

Ranker

The bridge between team-level fixture scores and player-level decisions: a player's own form multiplied by that player's fixture composite across the horizon, producing a ranked projected-value list.

Sortable by projected value, cost per point, price or minutes security; filterable by position, price threshold, team and minutes security. Permanent Value and Cost/Pt columns sit alongside average points per gameweek, the next fixture with its rank and score, and a per-gameweek fixture strip for each player. Players are listed under their full names rather than FPL's web_name, which is compact but ambiguous across a 700-player pool — and the column sorts on the string it displays rather than on a field nobody can see.

Dashboard

This week only. The Dashboard is deliberately locked to the single-gameweek horizon while every other view runs on the longer one, because captaincy and bench order are not multi-week questions.

It picks a captain by highest expected points among the players actually in the squad, orders the starting XI and bench, and raises risk flags — low minutes security, a brutal band, a low-confidence score. While a gameweek is in progress it reads the live endpoint and shows points as they land.

Planner

The most horizon-aware view, and the one furthest down the dependency chain: it depends on the Ranker and, through it, on the whole engine.

It takes the current squad, the Ranker's projections across the horizon, and the budget and free-transfer constraints, then evaluates every candidate out-and-in swap for its change in projected horizon score. Moves are ranked by gain per cost, with the four-point hit for an extra transfer modelled rather than ignored — the question being whether a move pays for its own hit across the horizon, not just in the week it is made. Triple Captain candidates are picked on the same expected-points basis the Dashboard uses for captaincy.
