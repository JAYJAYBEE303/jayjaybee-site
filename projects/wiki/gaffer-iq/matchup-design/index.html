There are several ways I could have made it easy for the user to see what they want to see. Do they want to see deep dives into specific matchups? Do they want to see what teams have strong or weak fixtures coming up? Does the user want to go through each gameweek and see what each matchup looks like? Do they want to see the key information worth holding onto for each gameweek, to see overall high and low potential weekends?

Well… why not all of it?

There are a lot of ways a user might want to see this information, so instead of having the page be number-heavy I went in the direction of something more graphical, interactive, and comprehensive to use and understand. The deep dive into a specific matchup sits in the centre, allowing for a comprehensive breakdown of what makes a matchup sway one way and by how much, with small info buttons the user will see to elaborate on a metric but which are not invasively spaced.

Two containers then sit left and right: the left shows all the matches by gameweek, the right shows all the matches upcoming by team. There is not a lot to expand on in either, so they sit nicely to one side with the information in the containers kept minimal.

The bottom strip provides the view with the best horizon, and this took a lot of work to get right — balancing interaction, animation, spacing and graphic indications. It is designed to highlight the notable matches when considering FPL specifically: strong and weak gameweeks, matches with extreme one-sided predictions, strong captaincy predictions, double gameweeks, postponed fixtures, and suggested best times to use an FPL chip. Getting the animations, spacing, colour coding and methods, general design philosophy and error presentation ironed out took roughly a million tokens of repeated tweaks, bug fixing and testing, but I think it is one of the most useful tools on the site for FPL users specifically.
